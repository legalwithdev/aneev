Low Mood & Depression — When to Get Help

VERIFIED SOURCES aneev AI · Mental Wellness Series · ~6 min read

Everyone feels sad sometimes — that is normal and it passes. Depression is different. It is a common medical condition (the WHO estimates around 322 million people live with it), and the most important thing to know is this: effective treatment exists for mild, moderate and severe depression. You do not have to push through alone.

Sadness vs depression — the key difference

The WHO describes a depressive episode as a depressed mood (feeling sad, irritable or empty) or a loss of pleasure or interest in activities, for most of the day, nearly every day, for at least two weeks — along with other symptoms. Ordinary sadness is usually tied to an event and lifts with time. Depression settles in and stays.

Common signs

  • Low, empty or irritable mood most of the day
  • Loss of interest or pleasure in things you used to enjoy
  • Sleep changed — too much or too little
  • Appetite or weight changed
  • Constant tiredness, low energy
  • Poor concentration, difficulty deciding
  • Excessive guilt, feeling worthless, hopelessness about the future
  • Thoughts about dying or suicide
If you recognise several of these and they have lasted two weeks or more, that is a signal to speak to a professional — not a reason to judge yourself. Depression is a health condition, like diabetes or high blood pressure.

What helps — day to day

  • Tell someone. Sharing with one trusted person — a friend, family member, teacher or doctor — is the single most useful first step.
  • One small step at a time. When everything feels heavy, shrink the goal: get up, wash, step outside. Small wins rebuild momentum.
  • Move a little. Even a short walk can lift mood. It is not a cure, but it genuinely helps.
  • Protect sleep. Fixed sleep and wake times; avoid long daytime naps; screens away before bed.
  • Be careful with alcohol and drugs. They feel like relief for a moment but make depression worse.
  • Be kind to yourself. Harsh self-talk deepens depression. Speak to yourself the way you would to a friend.
  • Try a self-help resource. The WHO's free Doing What Matters in Times of Stress teaches practical coping skills.

What actually treats depression

Two main approaches work, and a physician or psychiatrist decides what suits you:

  • Psychological treatment — talking therapy, including cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) and counselling.
  • Medicines — antidepressants, for moderate or severe depression, prescribed and monitored by a doctor.

Both are real, evidence-based treatments. Needing them is not a failure. Many people recover fully.

How to support someone who is struggling

  • Listen without fixing. "I'm here, tell me more" helps more than advice.
  • Take it seriously — don't dismiss it as "just stress" or "attention-seeking".
  • Stay in touch. A message or a walk together matters, even when they don't reply much.
  • Encourage professional help, and offer to help them find it or go along.
  • If they mention self-harm, stay with them and help them call a helpline or a doctor.
