How to Sleep Better — An Evidence-Based Guide

VERIFIED SOURCES aneev AI · Everyday Wellness Series · ~5 min read

Poor sleep shows up in everything — mood, focus, hunger, blood pressure. The good news: most sleep problems improve with a few consistent habits, not expensive gadgets. Every tip below comes from official public health guidance (US CDC, NHS UK, US NIH).

Signs your sleep needs work

  • Taking a long time to fall asleep, or waking up tired
  • Waking up several times at night
  • Daytime sleepiness, irritability, or trouble focusing
  • Relying on caffeine just to get through the afternoon

1. Keep a fixed sleep–wake time — every day

Go to bed and get up at about the same time daily, including weekends. Keeping the same schedule — with no more than about an hour of difference — helps your body clock settle. This is the single most effective habit.

2. Aim for 7–9 hours (adults)

The NHS says most healthy adults need around 7 to 9 hours a night. The US CDC recommends 7 or more hours for adults aged 18–60. The exact amount varies from person to person — the test is whether you feel rested in the day.

3. Make the room cool, dark and quiet

A cool, dark, quiet bedroom helps sleep. Use curtains, blinds, an eye mask or ear plugs if needed. Bright light in the hour before bed signals your brain to stay awake.

4. Screens off before bed

The US CDC suggests turning off electronic devices at least 30 minutes before bedtime. Bright screens and scrolling keep the mind alert, so the wind-down hour matters more than the exact device rule.

5. Watch your caffeine timing

Caffeine is a stimulant and its effects can last up to 8 hours. The NHS advises avoiding tea, coffee and alcohol for at least 6 hours before bed. If you are sensitive, keep caffeine to the morning.

6. Don't eat heavy meals or drink alcohol late

Avoid heavy or large meals within a few hours of bedtime (a light snack is fine), and avoid alcohol before bed — it may help you fall asleep but it disrupts sleep quality.

7. Move your body during the day

Regular daytime activity helps sleep. But timing matters: the NHS advises avoiding exercise in the 4 hours before bed, and the NIH suggests not exercising within about 3 hours of bedtime. Morning or early afternoon is ideal.

8. Keep naps short and early

If you nap, keep it short — the NIH advises adults nap for no more than 20 minutes, and to avoid long naps in the late afternoon or evening, since they make it harder to fall asleep at night.

Quick recap: same sleep time daily + 7–9 hours + cool dark quiet room + screens off before bed + caffeine cut-off + no heavy late meals + daytime exercise + short early naps. Consistency beats perfection.

When to see a physician

Talk to a qualified physician if you regularly have trouble sleeping, snore heavily or gasp during sleep, or feel extremely sleepy in the daytime despite enough time in bed.
